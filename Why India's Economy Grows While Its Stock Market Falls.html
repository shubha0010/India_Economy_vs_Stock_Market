Why India's Economy Grows While Its Stock Market Falls

A finance project on the gap between economic growth and index returns
Prepared by: Shubha Halder, October 2026

Summary. India's GDP grew 7.8% in the latest quarter, ahead of the RBI's 7.0% forecast, yet the Nifty is about 14% below its peak. This project argues there is no contradiction. A stock index measures a small, weighted basket of companies and looks forward, while GDP measures everything produced and looks backward. India's market fell because foreign investors sold its biggest stocks, index earnings disappointed for two years, three financial and energy heavyweights fell sharply, and the fastest-growing parts of the economy barely appear in the Nifty.

1. The puzzle

Most people assume that when an economy grows, share prices rise. In practice the two often separate. The United States shows the mirror image today: a slowing economy with record-high stocks. Understanding both cases shows that the economy and the stock market are different things.

2. Economy and market measure different things

  • The economy reports how businesses and households are doing today. Its data looks backward.
  • The stock market prices what investors expect companies to earn in the future.
  • Evidence from Covid: in 2020 the market recovered in about 4 months, while the economy took about 16.

An index is also not "the market." It is a selected basket, weighted by company size. If a few giants dominate it, they decide its direction.

3. Four reasons India's market is falling

Reason 1: Foreign money is leaving

Foreign institutional investors (FIIs) are selling because of high US bond yields, a falling rupee and geopolitical risk. Domestic institutions bought heavily in March, even more than FIIs sold, yet the Nifty still fell about 11% in two months. The explanation is where the money went. FIIs sold the biggest Nifty names such as Reliance, HDFC Bank and Infosys, while domestic SIP money flowed mostly into mid- and small-cap funds.

Total money coming in does not matter if it buys different stocks from the ones being sold.

Reason 2: Nifty earnings disappointed

For two years, Nifty earnings grew only in low single digits while investors expected double digits. The last quarter beat expectations, but analysts barely raised their forecasts for the future.

Markets price the future. One good quarter does not repair two years of missed expectations.

Reason 3: The biggest sectors are struggling

Financials and IT together make up about 45% of the Nifty. Bank Nifty is down about 10% this year and Nifty IT is down about 27%. Just three stocks, HDFC Bank, ICICI Bank and Reliance, carry about 27% of the index, and HDFC Bank and Reliance are each down more than 25%.

Reason 4: The Nifty does not mirror India's economy

The sectors driving growth, such as capital goods, electrical equipment, electronics and machinery, have almost no weight in the Nifty. India is now the world's second-largest mobile phone maker, but winners like Dixon (+34%) and Amber (+21%) are not in the index.

The economy's growth is happening in companies the index does not hold.

4. The same lesson in reverse: the United States

The US economy grew only 1.5% last quarter against a long-term average near 3.2%, with inflation at 3.4%. Yet the S&P 500 is up about 17% in a year. Big Tech drives the index, 86% of companies beat earnings estimates (versus a 5-year average of 78%), and passive funds, buybacks and zero-day options all push money into the same few stocks. About a third of an S&P 500 purchase goes into the Magnificent 7.

5. Findings

  • Markets price future earnings, while economies measure what has already happened.
  • An index is a selected, weighted basket, a proxy and not the whole picture.
  • When strength or weakness sits in a few names, the index and the economy can tell opposite stories.
  • India's growth is concentrated in sectors the Nifty barely holds, while the Nifty's largest sectors are the ones under pressure.

Conclusion. Strong GDP and a falling Nifty are consistent once you ask which few companies are actually doing the moving. Investors who want exposure to India's growth story may need to look beyond the headline index, though this project does not recommend any specific investment.
